\begin{abstract}
Vision-language-action models (VLAs) succeed on trained tasks and fail when a new instruction pairs a known object with a known target. We map these failures into an atlas and ask what repairs each one. On five frozen VLAs, the failures fall into a few kinds (the place follows a cue, the held outline picks the carry, the habitual object is grasped, relational language is ignored, a covered target is lost). A causal cue edit routes the place, the carry and, more weakly, the grasp. Every repair that worked in our tests places an explicit component outside the trained weights: an input edit (painted views of the active entity), an external transport (ReGuide), a resolver, a pose memory or a servo. No repair through training alone reached it under our recipe (frozen VLM, 6k-step action-expert fine-tune, LIBERO). On \pifive, a new input pathway for the exact target stays unread (learned where-share \mi.001 without a servo) unless the data force it, and the best servo-free route trails the servo-kept policy by \mi.100 [\mi.261, +.050] on development states and \mi.086 [\mi.244, +.056] on a new held-out set, not significantly. On \pizero{} (one seed on the first sets), the network reads the target (.542 [.478, .609]) and still fails more held-out pairings than its servo-kept twin, on development states (\mi.256 [\mi.386, \mi.128]) and on two new pre-registered held-out sets (\mi.222 [\mi.383, \mi.069]; \mi.208 [\mi.317, \mi.100]). Most of the servo's gain comes from objects that end 10 cm or more from the target and not at a known partner's target. In a system we built, removing the resolver or the pose memory restores its failure, and a where-module inside a retrained policy and an external one land within a few points of each other (.883 to .931 on three held-out sets; non-inferiority not shown). Removing the servo from the retrained policy costs .678 [.492, .864] on held-out pairings, but it falls far below a network trained without a servo (\mi.503 [\mi.806, \mi.217]), so this removal shows dependence of the retrained expert and not that a network cannot own the where. A separate learned owner beats the in-network route on \pizero{} across four seeds (+.191 [+.107, +.267]) and comes within .04 of the fitted servo. On \pifive{} a single-seed learned head is not significant (+.089 [\mi.208, +.346]). The \pizero{} servo contrast now replicates on three new held-out sets. Published in-network fixes that retrain on data covering the pairings (CRAFT, ACT-VLA) report comparable success and lie outside our recipe, so the necessity of an explicit owner is an empirical claim, not a proof. All results come from LIBERO, with one training seed per policy (two for RAA-sL; four for the \pizero{} arms of the last set), mostly development states (one new held-out set for \pizero{} and the removal ladder), privileged perception inputs and a multiplicity correction only over the three primaries of that set.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A household robot that stacks a cup on a plate and a bowl on a shelf should also put the cup on the shelf. Vision-language-action models (VLAs) reach .98 to 1.00 success on the trained tasks of scene 1 (\pifive{} and GR00T) and fail on new pairings of the same objects and targets \citep{craft2610,liberopro2510,liberocf2602}.

The diagnosis is well established. A VLA keeps its local manipulation skill and loses the global grounding of the instruction. ``Correcting WHERE, Preserving HOW'' (ReGuide; \citealp{reguide2609}) states this division of labour in its title and lifts several backbones with a training-free wrapper that rebinds the referent. \citet{jiang2610} find that the instruction's target is linearly decodable in the network and goes unused (encoded but not in control). We take this diagnosis as the starting point and ask how many distinct failures it covers, what routes each one, and what repairs it.

Our answer is an atlas. For each failure we report where it appears across five VLAs, the cue that routes the wrong outcome (a causal test), a same-state reference planner that shows the input holds the needed information, and a row of repairs: more data, the target as an input, and an explicit module. The atlas rests on one hypothesis about why the repair columns differ. Behaviour cloning has no reason to build a function that the trained tasks never need, because the role phrase, the painted views and the held object already determine the target there. This is the causal-confusion pattern of \citet{dehaan1905}. A function that the trained tasks never demand therefore tends to stay unlearned, and training alone may not supply it. We test this hypothesis in one recipe only (frozen VLM, 6k-step action-expert fine-tune, LIBERO); other published recipes retrain in-network and may succeed (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The atlas. Failures of five frozen VLAs on new pairings, each with a causal cue test, a same-state reference planner and an excess-over-partners measure, and a pre-registered cue law for one scene (Section~\ref{sec:atlas}).
\item A function for each failure. A separable decomposition (resolver, pose memory, where-module) owns the functions that the failures lack. In the system we built, removing the resolver or the memory restores its failure on three held-out state sets (set 2 runs a module variant; the resolver's cross terms hold by construction), with two seeds where available (Section~\ref{sec:functions}).
\item A repair matrix. Every repair that worked places an explicit component outside the trained weights, and no repair through training alone reached it under our recipe. A dose-by-servo grid on \pifive{} (development states, one seed per route) shows that a new target pathway stays unread unless the data force it, and the best servo-free route is not significantly below the servo-kept policy (\mi.100 [\mi.261, +.050]; \mi.086 on a new held-out set). On \pizero{} (one seed) the network reads the target (.542) and still fails more held-out pairings than its servo-kept twin (\mi.256 on development states, \mi.222 [\mi.383, \mi.069] on a new pre-registered held-out set), a pattern we call read but not in control. A separate owner of the where, learned from the demonstrations (H*) or fitted (the servo), beats letting the network learn it on \pizero{} (H* minus A0 +.191 [+.107, +.267] over four seeds). \citet{jiang2610} find a target that is encoded and unused; in our case the token is read and not in control. Explicit components (our servo-based where-module W5, ReGuide, an input-only front end) repair the failures (Sections~\ref{sec:inside} to~\ref{sec:matrix}). The necessity of an explicit owner is an empirical claim over our recipe, and published in-network fixes lie outside it.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Scope.} LIBERO, two scenes, one seed per policy (two for RAA-sL), mostly development states and one new held-out set; Section~\ref{sec:limits} lists the privileged inputs.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Object-centric architectures \citep{taxpose2211,spot2411,foci2609,oawam2605,potvla2607}, a shortcut audit of LIBERO \citep{jiangaudit2606} and a steerability predictor that failed its registered test (Appendix~\ref{app:G}) are discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:L0}.

\textbf{Diagnosing held-out pairings.} LIBERO-PRO, LIBERO-CF and LIBERO-Plus \citep{liberoplus2510} show collapse under perturbed objects and instructions. \citet{emukpere2602} separate intact skills from brittle target selection. CRAFT reports 8.4\%, 2.8\% and 1.1\% success on undemonstrated combinations of its Pick--Place benchmark for \pifive, \pizero{} and GR00T N1.7 after plain full fine-tuning (its Table~1). \citet{schmalz2026} trace the same failure on SmolVLA to partial composition: wrong destinations go to places the object was paired with before. \citet{gerigk2610} and \citet{cofact2608} study visual shortcuts in VLAs, \citet{better2604} give a failure taxonomy with architectural attributions, and \citet{robocombine2610} measure recombination limits at hand-off states. The atlas adds a causal cue test, a same-state reference planner and repair columns.

\textbf{Where and how.} ReGuide corrects where the arm goes with grounded poses and hands the frozen policy back control. We add that a where-module inside a retrained policy lands within a few points of the same success, and that under our recipe the training-only routes did not reach it. Other fixes retrain in-network (CRAFT, ACT-VLA, \citealp{actvla2607}; AC-VLA, \citealp{acvla2607}; task scrubbing, \citealp{gerigk2610}) or edit a frozen policy (LiLo-VLA, \citealp{lilovla2602}; Harness VLA, \citealp{harnessvla2607}; VP-VLA, \citealp{vpvla2603}; counterfactual guidance, \citealp{cofact2608}). Those in-network fixes report large gains on their own splits (CRAFT: 8.4\% to 84.2\% for \pifive, 2.8\% to 64.3\% for \pizero) and lie outside our recipe, so our necessity claim does not cover them. ThinkProprio \citep{thinkproprio2602} and ReViP \citep{revip2601} study how a VLA uses its state input, which bears on our \pifive{} versus \pizero{} contrast. w$^{2}$VLA \citep{w2vla2606} conditions the robot-state tokens on skill and object strings through FiLM.

\textbf{Mechanisms and shortcuts.} \citet{grant2603} and \citet{wulff2610} patch activations in VLAs (Appendix~\ref{app:B}). Shortcut learning \citep{geirhos2020} and copycat behaviour cloning \citep{wen2020} are the general forms of the confusion that we test for.

\section{Setting, measures and the atlas protocol}
\label{sec:setting}

\textbf{Models and scenes.} We test five frozen VLAs trained on LIBERO's 40 tasks \citep{libero2306} (\pifive, \pizero, GR00T N1, X-VLA and SmolVLA; \citealp{pi05_2504,pi0_2410,gr00tn1_2503,xvla2510,smolvla2506}) and never retrain them in the atlas. Scene 1 descends from LIBERO-Goal's kitchen and has trained tasks K1--K6 and six held-out pairings (N1--N6); the atlas tasks are K1--K4 and K6, and K5 (the rack task) appears in the module and seed analyses. The retrains of Section~\ref{sec:ladder} train on LIBERO-Goal's eight known tasks. Scene 2 is LIBERO-10's living-room scene with two mugs and two plates and one trained task. A held-out pairing combines an object role from one trained task with a target role from another, and its known partners are the trained tasks that share its object and its target. A cue is any visual feature that covaried with a sub-goal in training.

\textbf{Atlas protocol.} A failure enters the atlas when three conditions hold. First, the excess failure E on held-out pairings is positive, where E is the failure rate of the pairing minus the failure rate of its known partners, so that E isolates the pairing. Second, a scripted reference planner that reads simulator state reaches .80 on the same states (.894 on the held-out pairings of scene 1), which shows that the input holds the information the task needs. Third, a causal cue test names the routing cue: we edit or move the candidate cue and the outcome follows the edit. A failure that misses the second condition belongs to the task, and we report it as a limit. Scene-2 F1 misses the planner threshold on one pairing (the planner pools .65 [.25, 1.00] and scores .333 on N1), so we keep it as a pooled observation and not as a full entry.

\textbf{Learned where-share.} For the repair columns we measure how much a network's behaviour depends on a given input. We replay recorded model inputs open loop and patch the where-input (the state token, or token plus crop) from a counterfactual run, in which the target is shifted, into the original run. The where-share is the fraction of the counterfactual effect on the output chunk that this patch recovers. A route passes when the lower bound of the pooled share is at least .70, the prefix share is below RAA-sL's (.089) and the effect is positive; it fails when the upper bound is under .30. No route reached .70, so the prefix condition decided no verdict (Appendix~\ref{app:E}).

\textbf{Statistics.} Each confirmatory test has a pre-registered record that fixes arms, states, predictions and decision rules before any main rollout. We compute every contrast by a paired bootstrap that resamples tasks and states jointly, with 2,000 draws, and report 95\% intervals. Held-out state sets are start states that no earlier experiment used; reads on other states are development reads, marked as such.

\section{The atlas: failures and their cues}
\label{sec:atlas}

This section lists what fails and which cue routes each failure, with each entry kept to its cue, its causal test and its effect (Appendix~\ref{app:L3} holds the full detail; Figure~\ref{fig:gallery} shows each failure in frames, and Figure~\ref{fig:atlas} and Table~\ref{tab:B1} give the measured break of each failure on all five models).

\textbf{F1, the place follows a cue.} After a correct grasp, the carry and release follow a cue and ignore the instruction's target role. The excess failure on held-out states of scene 1 is +.450 [.28, .61] for \pifive, +.225 [.05, .43] for \pizero, +.500 [.26, .74] for GR00T and +.379 [.04, .73] for X-VLA. The cue test removes the cue by input edit: role views (silhouettes of the active role's entity painted into the camera images) plus role phrases (the instruction restated per role) lift \pifive{} from .450 to .817 on the development states of the cue test. A further front end, tvpb, adds the held object painted as its box and reaches .938 against .454 for frozen \pifive{} on 40 new states, equivalent to ReGuide (.929) with no cost on trained tasks (Appendix~\ref{app:B}). The state sets differ, so .450 and .454 are two measurements of the same frozen model. E is an excess failure rate and is not a success rate.

\textbf{F1m, the held outline starts the carry.} On \pifive, the high carry follows the outline of the held object. Painting the held bottle as its box raises N4+N6 from .625 to .812 (+.188 [+.075, +.325]) with no cost on trained tasks.

\textbf{F2, the habitual object is grasped.} The grasp excess is +.650 [.39, .88] on \pizero{} and +.833 [.67, .96] on SmolVLA. Role views alone lift \pizero{} by +.183 [+.021, +.412].

\textbf{F7, relational language is ignored.} On \pifive{} and GR00T the relational choice equals the ambiguous choice on 92.5 to 100\% of states. A resolver that turns the relation into one entity lifts \pifive{} from .40 to 1.00 on the spatial family (SP, ``the left bowl'') and from .20 to .775 on the negated family (NG, ``the bowl that is not on the plate''). On \pizero, X-VLA and SmolVLA we cannot test F7, because they fail even the named-bowl control (0 of 20).

\textbf{F6, a covered target is lost.} A cylinder covers a displaced plate. The cover costs \pifive{} placement precision (cost .38 [.28, .48]; 44 of its 50 failures release within 3 cm of the plate centre) and GR00T the target's location (cost .60 [.51, .68]), and a control with the cylinder beside the plate keeps \pifive{} at .967.

\textbf{Scene 2 and a pre-registered cue law.} In scene 2, none of the 720 episodes on the two held-out pairings succeeds (\pifive, \pizero{} and GR00T, the last below its testability floor; X-VLA and SmolVLA have no scene-2 entry). A pre-registered test on new layouts confirms a majority rule on \pifive: the place goes to the plate backed by two of three cues (nearer, more salient, trained), on .912 [.883, .942] of 445 placements in which the cues disagree. The views that repair scene 1 give .000 here. In scene 2 the cue is position, which the grasp needs, so painting it out removes information that the task requires, and an input edit helps only when the cue is not needed.

\textbf{A moved plate.} Frozen \pifive{} follows a moved plate (.975 [.925, 1.000]); the narrow retrain R2 does not (0 of 40), so retraining induces this failure (Appendices~\ref{app:F} and~\ref{app:L3}).

\section{Where each failure sits: functions and a separable decomposition}
\label{sec:functions}

If a failure is a missing function, a module that owns the function should repair the failure and its removal should return it.

\textbf{Four functions.} F1 and F1m lack an owner of where the object goes and when it is let go. F2 lacks a binding from the named object role to the object that the gripper approaches. F7 lacks a resolution of a relation to one entity. F6 lacks, in our retrained models, a memory of a target across queries (frozen \pifive{} loses placement precision instead). In a standard VLA these functions share one path, from the VLM prefix to the action expert.

\textbf{The effect does not localise to one write or band, and the expert reads the prefix at layer 14.} Activation patching on frozen \pifive{} finds no single write that carries the effect (largest .189 [.167, .209]), no selective band of layers, and a cliff in the residual stream from 1.21 at layer 13 to .10 at layer 14, a narrow read site at the keys and values of expert layer 14 (Appendix~\ref{app:D}).

\textbf{A separable decomposition.} Three explicit modules own the functions. The resolver R turns a relational instruction such as ``the bowl on the left'' into one entity and serves F7. The pose memory M keeps the last estimate of an entity that a cover hides or a view loses and serves F6. The where-module serves F1 and F1m by aiming the arm at the release point of the target; Section~\ref{sec:modules} builds it. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the decomposition on the same frozen \pifive.

\textbf{Removal test.} We run three arms on the same states, with RAA-sL-W5 as the policy: full, no resolver and no memory. The contrasts are the gain of the resolver on the relational cells, the gain of the memory on the covered-plate cell, and the two cross terms (design in Appendix~\ref{app:L2}; Figure~\ref{fig:removal} and Table~\ref{tab:removal}, Appendix~\ref{app:C}).

\textbf{Reading.} Removing the resolver restores the relational failure, removing the memory restores the hidden-target failure, and neither removal moves the other cell (cross terms within an equivalence bound on all three sets). The resolver's cross terms hold by construction, because the resolver does not engage on the other cells (identical on all 60 states); only the memory's cross terms are measurements. The where-module has its own removal ladder (Section~\ref{sec:modules}). The modules exist to repair these failures, so the test verifies separability in a system we built, and the memory result is a location-memory result in the RAA models that says nothing about memory in frozen \pifive{} (three caveats, Appendix~\ref{app:L2}).

\section{Repairing inside the network}
\label{sec:inside}

If the missing function could be learned inside the network, the repair would be a training recipe. This section tests three recipes: retraining at matched data with a role channel, giving the network the exact target as an input at several data doses, and the same on \pizero, whose state pathway is trained.

\subsection{The matched-data ladder}
\label{sec:ladder}

We first train the binding into a policy on the same demonstrations as a plain retrain and test whether it closes the place gap. A ladder of retrains on LIBERO-Goal's eight known tasks uses the same 52,181 frames, the frozen VLM, the same action expert, 6,001 steps and one seed. R0 retrains the action expert on whole instructions and frames. R2 restricts the input to the active role's entity, with role phrases and painted views. Rrel is R0 plus the role channel, and RAA is R2 plus it. RAA-c feeds the channel as a continuous state token instead of text, RAA-s adds a fixed servo term, and the suffix L (RAA-L, RAA-sL) starts from the broader LIBERO-fine-tuned initialisation. The channel is the position of the active role entity relative to the gripper, in whole centimetres, entered as a short text suffix on the prompt because the served \pifive{} reads no state input.

\textbf{Input purity repairs the grasp and the channel repairs part of the place.} R2 fixes the grasp (right-grasp rate .972) and places only .400 of those right grasps. RAA beats R2 on held-out pairings by +.267 [+.092, +.467], and its right-grasp rate rises by only .028, so the place step supplies almost all of the gain. The trained tasks do not fall; they rise from .753 (R2) to .839 (RAA), +.086 [+.011, +.172]. The channel needs pure input: Rrel gains +.236 [\mi.006, +.553] over R0, and RAA beats Rrel by +.378 [+.133, +.658].

\textbf{The remaining gap sits at the release.} In 47 of 51 failures, RAA carries the right object to the right target and lets go outside LIBERO's placement tolerance, with misses of 3.7 to 7.9 cm against ReGuide's median of 0.9 to 1.7 cm. A fixed servo term lifts RAA to .717 (RAA-s), and the broader LIBERO-fine-tuned initialisation gives .878 (RAA-sL, two seeds: .878, .822; the step from RAA-s is not supported, +.161 [\mi.017, +.311]). The servo gain raises the question of whether the network reads the number at all (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}, Appendices~\ref{app:H} and~\ref{app:L1}).

\subsection{The dose-by-servo grid on \pifive}
\label{sec:grid}

In RAA-sL, patching the servo input and the state token together accounts for .871 [.834, .914] of the total effect over 124 development observations. Patching the servo input alone gives .878 and the token alone \mi.007, so the pair sums to .871 (the prefix carries .132). (Table~\ref{tab:J1}'s .920 is the servo-alone share on the 96 N and K observations of the seed comparison, which exclude F7; the equality of .878 with RAA-sL's held-out success is a coincidence.) The token's \mi.007 means that the trained \pifive{} expert does not read the value of the newly added token, and a second training seed reproduces this (\mi.002 [\mi.0024, \mi.0017]). Our shortcut hypothesis (Section~\ref{sec:intro}) predicts that a network uses a where-signal only as far as the data force it to and only if no other path supplies the correction. Three routes (A, B, A+), one matched comparator (A0) and one post hoc arm (A+0) test it, with one selection rule fixed before any run (Section~\ref{sec:setting}).

\textbf{Routes.} Route A relabels held-phase demonstration frames with a counterfactual goal offset of 1 to 9 cm on a servo-free model, so only the token can supply the shift. Route A0 is the same model with no relabel. Route B adds a crop of the target as an extra image input. Route A+ adds planner demonstrations with shifts of 19 to 38 cm and keeps the servo; A+0 is A+ without the servo, added post hoc (Table~\ref{tab:share}, Appendix~\ref{app:E}). A control with the planner data, no token and no servo falls below RAA-sL by \mi.247 [\mi.483, \mi.030].

\textbf{A newly added pathway stays unread without a dose, and the servo shuts it out.} With no data dose the share is zero (A0 and B). The relabel raises it by +.272 [+.238, +.312] over A0, and the crop adds nothing detectable (+.030 [\mi.034, +.089]). With the servo kept, even planner shifts of 19 to 38 cm leave the share at the reference value (A+ \mi.007); A+0 reads .124, +.133 [+.096, +.171] above A+, and does not do better than the small relabel (A+0 minus A \mi.150 [\mi.196, \mi.104]). No route reaches the .70 bar. Behaviour on held-out pairings (development states, one seed per route) puts every servo-free route below RAA-sL (.878): A .778 (\mi.100 [\mi.261, +.050]) and A0 (\mi.192 [\mi.406, +.019]) are not significantly below it, while B (\mi.233 [\mi.447, \mi.019]) and A+0 (.603, \mi.275) are. The best servo-free route, A, trails the servo-kept policy by a margin that the data do not separate from zero, and a new held-out set agrees (A \mi.086 [\mi.244, +.056], A0 \mi.167 [\mi.356, +.017]; Appendices~\ref{app:E} and~\ref{app:M}).

\textbf{An oracle.} On 131 observations on 23 layouts where only the token names the target's table spot, A+0 and A follow the token ($\varphi$ .771 [.605, .887] and .662 [.542, .783], against \mi.001 for A0), while with the servo kept the network follows it on no frame (A+ \mi.068, RAA-sL \mi.006). The layer can be learned when the data force it, and the servo carries the where (Appendix~\ref{app:L4}).

\subsection{\pizero: the network reads the target and still fails more pairings}
\label{sec:pi0}

The \pifive{} null concerns a pathway that we add, so we repeat the test on \pizero, which reads state natively. We put the same token into its trained state dimensions 0 to 3 and train two arms: servo kept, and servo off with no added data (Table~\ref{tab:L2}, Appendix~\ref{app:L4}; Section~\ref{sec:limits} lists the deviations from \pizero{} as shipped).
\textbf{The \pifive{} null is specific to a newly added pathway.} With the servo off, \pizero{} reads the token at .542 [.478, .609], which is +.531 [+.463, +.607] above \pifive's A0. With the servo kept, the share falls to \mi.150, so the servo suppresses the token here too.

\textbf{Reading the token does not give control.} The servo-off arm scores .506 on held-out pairings against .761 with the servo kept, a difference of \mi.256 [\mi.386, \mi.128], with the trained tasks intact (+.094 [+.003, +.208], non-inferior at the margin of \mi.05). A rule we fixed before the rollouts holds. We call this pattern read but not in control. It differs from the encoded-but-unused target of \citet{jiang2610}: here the network uses the token (share .542) and still places worse than the same network with an explicit servo. The contrast compares a network with the same network plus a fixed-form controller on the same token (its gain is fitted to the demonstrations).

\textbf{The result replicates on a new pre-registered held-out set.} On a fifth set of start states that no earlier experiment used, the servo-off arm scores .517 and the servo-kept arm .739 on held-out pairings: \mi.222 [\mi.383, \mi.069], with the trained tasks non-inferior (+.078 [\mi.019, +.228]). It survives a Holm correction over the three registered primaries (adjusted one-sided $p = .003$,, with h1 counted at its own $p$) and concentrates in the bottle pairings (\mi.383 [\mi.533, \mi.233]; cheese \mi.061 [\mi.172, +.045]). A sixth held-out set gives \mi.208 [\mi.317, \mi.100], a third replication (Appendix~\ref{app:M5}). It still rests on one training seed (Appendix~\ref{app:M}).

\section{Repairing with explicit modules}
\label{sec:modules}

If the network does not own the where, a module must. We give a module sole ownership of the target-directed motion of the same RAA-sL checkpoint at serving time and train nothing. The module owns where the object goes and when it is let go, and the expert keeps how to move: the lift, the approach, the final alignment and the gripper. The final design, W5, aims at the release point that the demonstrations show for the target's class and hands the last 3 cm back to the expert (Appendix~\ref{app:L5}).

\textbf{W5 and ReGuide land within a few points of each other} (.892, .931, .894 against .914, .883, .917 on three held-out sets; non-inferiority not shown; Appendix~\ref{app:M4}).


\textbf{A removal ladder on a new held-out set.} Removing the servo from the W5 policy lowers held-out success by .678 [.492, .864], but noW also falls \mi.503 [\mi.806, \mi.217] below a network trained without a servo (A0) and collapses on the trained tasks (.272 against .956). This removal shows that the retrained expert depends on the module and leaves open whether a network can own the where (Appendix~\ref{app:M4}).

\textbf{A learned separate owner.} Does a separate learned module that owns the where, with no hand-chosen form, beat letting the action expert learn the same geometry from the same data? On \pizero{}, H* (the servo law aimed at a release point learned from the demonstrations, in a module outside the network) scores .674 on a seventh held-out set over four seeds, against .483 for A0 and .710 with the servo: +.191 [+.107, +.267] over A0 (Holm-adjusted $p$ .001), positive on every seed, and \mi.036 [\mi.103, +.028] against the servo (non-inferiority at \mi.10 not shown). The servo contrast replicates here (\mi.227 [\mi.287, \mi.164]). Earlier single-seed reads were underpowered: H* on a sixth set (+.094 [\mi.010, +.199]) and, on \pifive{}, the head H (+.089 [\mi.208, +.346]; Appendix~\ref{app:M3}). On \pizero{} the data favour a separate owner over the in-network route; on \pifive{} a learned form stays not shown (Appendix~\ref{app:M5},~\ref{app:M6}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Privileged inputs.} The fixes need depth and calibration: the entity estimator and ReGuide's runs use rendered depth, scene-2 role views use simulator masks, and the scene-2 where channel has an error of 0 by construction. The F7 resolver is a fixed-template rule on a parsed scene, and the reference planners use simulator state.
\textbf{The \pizero{} arms.} Our \pizero{} arms deviate from \pizero{} as shipped: the token overwrites state dimensions 0 to 3 (trained on end-effector position and one rotation component), and the arms use quantile normalisation and no delta actions; the servo-kept share of \mi.150 is leftover dependence on those columns (Appendix~\ref{app:K6}). The \mi.256 gap on development states replicates on two new held-out sets (\mi.222, \mi.208), where the servo constants have no home advantage, and all rest on one training seed.
\textbf{Baselines, statistics and scale.} Our ReGuide is a reimplementation with our segmenter, and we ran no arms on the CRAFT, ACT-VLA or AC-VLA splits, so our numbers are not comparable with theirs (Appendix~\ref{app:L0}). Every trained policy has one seed (E4 has intervals of about $\pm.3$), except RAA-sL (two; Appendix~\ref{app:J}) and the three \pizero{} arms of the seventh set (four; Appendix~\ref{app:M6}); \pifive{} stays single-seed; its seed gaps (.056 on held-out pairings, .081 on trained tasks) show that differences of that size between single-seed policies are within seed noise. The designs W1 to W5 came from diagnoses on development states, and two of the three pooled held-out sets were seen before registration (Appendix~\ref{app:L5}). We apply no multiplicity correction to the dozens of registered tests, except Holm over the three primaries of the new set. The atlas runs mostly on development states, and only F1 and F2 replicate on held-out states; the \pizero{} contrast and the removal ladder also have the new set. ``Explicit owner'' in our results also means added information that an RGB-only VLA lacks. Our necessity claim covers a frozen-VLM, 6k-step action-expert fine-tune on LIBERO.

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

VLAs fail new pairings of known parts in a few identifiable ways. Every repair that worked in our tests places an explicit component outside the trained weights, and no repair through training alone reached it under our recipe: on \pifive{} a new input pathway stays unread unless the data force it, and \pizero{} reads the target (.542) and trails its servo-kept twin by .256 on development states and by .222, .208 and .227 on three new held-out sets; a separate learned owner beats the in-network route by +.191 [+.107, +.267] over four seeds and lands within .04 of the servo; on \pifive{} a single-seed learned head is not significant. Published in-network fixes lie outside our recipe, so the necessity of an explicit owner is an empirical claim over our tests.

\section*{Impact Statement}

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine Learning. Robot policies that fail on new combinations of instructions, objects and targets can act in ways that a user did not intend, such as carrying an object to the wrong place, and such failures matter for the safety of robots that work near people. Our atlas helps practitioners locate and test for these failures before deployment, and our repairs depend on privileged perception that a deployed system may lack. A policy that passes our tests in simulation is not certified safe on a real robot. We see no further societal consequences that we must highlight here.
