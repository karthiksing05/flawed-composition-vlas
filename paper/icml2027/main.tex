\begin{abstract}
Vision-language-action models (VLAs) succeed on trained tasks and fail when a new instruction pairs a known object with a known target. We map these failures into an atlas and ask what repairs each one. On five VLAs, the failures fall into a few kinds (the place follows a cue, the held outline picks the carry, the habitual object is grasped, relational language is ignored, a covered target is lost), and a causal cue test routes each one. Each failure sits in a function that a standard VLA spreads through one learned network. Explicit modules in a separable decomposition own these functions, and removing a module returns exactly its failure on three held-out state sets. Repairs inside the network fall short. On \pifive, a new input pathway for the exact target stays unread (learned where-share \mi.007) unless the data force it. On \pizero, whose state pathway is trained, the network reads the target (.542 [.478, .609]) and still fails more held-out pairings than with an explicit where-owner (\mi.256 [\mi.386, \mi.128]). Explicit modules repair the failures and take over the function: a where-module inside a retrained policy and an external one converge at .89 to .92 on held-out pairings. All results come from LIBERO, with one training seed per policy (two for RAA-sL) and privileged perception inputs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A household robot that stacks a cup on a plate and a bowl on a shelf should also put the cup on the shelf. Vision-language-action models (VLAs) reach .98 to 1.00 success on the tasks they trained on and fail on new pairings of the same objects and targets \citep{craft2610,liberopro2510,liberocf2602}.

The diagnosis is well established. A VLA keeps its local manipulation skill and loses the global grounding of the instruction. ``Correcting WHERE, Preserving HOW'' (ReGuide; \citealp{reguide2609}) states this division of labour in its title and lifts several backbones with a training-free wrapper that rebinds the referent. \citet{jiang2610} find that the instruction's target is linearly decodable in the network and goes unused. We take this diagnosis as the starting point and ask how many distinct failures it covers, what routes each one, and what repairs it.

Our answer is an atlas. For each failure we report where it appears across five VLAs, the cue that routes the wrong outcome (a causal test), a same-state reference planner that shows the input holds the needed information, and a row of repairs: more data, the target as an input, and an explicit module. The atlas rests on one hypothesis about why the repair columns differ. Behaviour cloning has no reason to build a function that the trained tasks never need, because the role phrase, the painted views and the held object already determine the target there. This is the causal-confusion pattern of \citet{dehaan1905}. A function that a standard VLA spreads through one learned network therefore resists repair from inside that network.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The atlas. Failures of five frozen VLAs on new pairings, each with a causal cue test, a same-state reference planner and an excess-over-partners measure, and a pre-registered cue law for one scene (Section~\ref{sec:atlas}).
\item A function for each failure. A separable decomposition (resolver, pose memory, where-module) owns the functions that the failures lack, and removing a module returns exactly its failure on three held-out state sets, with two seeds where available (Section~\ref{sec:functions}).
\item A repair matrix. Repairs inside the network fall short: a dose-by-servo grid on \pifive{} shows that a new target pathway stays unread unless the data force it, and on \pizero{} the network reads the target (.542) and still fails more held-out pairings than with an explicit where-owner (``read but not in control'', which extends the finding of \citealt{jiang2610} from internal representations to an explicit input). Explicit modules (our servo-based where-module W5, and ReGuide) repair the failure, and the servo takes over the function (Sections~\ref{sec:inside} to~\ref{sec:matrix}). We state this necessity as an empirical claim over the tests we ran. It carries no proof.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Scope.} All results come from LIBERO, two scenes, with one seed per trained policy (two for RAA-sL); Section~\ref{sec:limits} lists the privileged inputs.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Object-centric architectures \citep{taxpose2211,spot2411,foci2609,oawam2605,potvla2607}, a shortcut audit of LIBERO \citep{jiangaudit2606} and a steerability predictor that failed its registered test (Appendix~\ref{app:G}) are discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:L0}.

\textbf{Diagnosing held-out pairings.} LIBERO-PRO, LIBERO-CF and LIBERO-Plus \citep{liberoplus2510} show collapse under perturbed objects and instructions. \citet{emukpere2602} separate intact skills from brittle target selection. CRAFT reports 8.4\%, 2.8\% and 1.1\% on held-out pairings for \pifive, \pizero{} and GR00T after plain fine-tuning. The atlas adds a causal cue test, a reference planner and repair columns.

\textbf{Where and how.} ReGuide owns the framing of this paper's thesis: it corrects where the arm goes with grounded poses and hands the frozen policy back control. ReGuide shows that an external where-correction works. We add that an explicit where-module inside a retrained policy converges to the same success, and that repairs inside the network fall short even when the network reads the target. Other fixes retrain (CRAFT, ACT-VLA, \citealp{actvla2607}; AC-VLA, \citealp{acvla2607}) or edit a frozen policy (LiLo-VLA, \citealp{lilovla2602}; Harness VLA, \citealp{harnessvla2607}; VP-VLA, \citealp{vpvla2603}). w2VLA \citep{w2vla2606} conditions the robot-state tokens on separately embedded skill and object strings through FiLM.

\textbf{Mechanisms and shortcuts.} \citet{grant2603} inject one task's activations into the action expert while the policy views another scene; the injected task's motor trajectory wins in 99.6\% of 1,968 \pifive{} episodes, the destination task succeeds on 2.6\%, and the effect is weaker on GR00T (57.0\%) and SmolVLA. They read the motor program as bound to absolute workspace coordinates. \citet{wulff2610} find patching sensitivity spread across layers, which agrees with Section~\ref{sec:functions}.

\section{Setting, measures and the atlas protocol}
\label{sec:setting}

\textbf{Models and scenes.} We test five frozen VLAs trained on LIBERO's 40 tasks \citep{libero2306} (\pifive, \pizero, GR00T N1, X-VLA and SmolVLA; \citealp{pi05_2504,pi0_2410,gr00tn1_2503,xvla2510,smolvla2506}) and never retrain them in the atlas. Scene 1 descends from LIBERO-Goal's kitchen and has five trained tasks (K1--K4, K6) and six held-out pairings (N1--N6). Scene 2 is LIBERO-10's living-room scene with two mugs and two plates and one trained task. A held-out pairing combines an object role from one trained task with a target role from another, and its known partners are the trained tasks that share its object and its target. A cue is any visual feature that covaried with a sub-goal in training.

\textbf{Atlas protocol.} A failure enters the atlas when three conditions hold. First, the excess failure E on held-out pairings is positive, where E is the failure rate of the pairing minus the failure rate of its known partners, so that E isolates the pairing. Second, a scripted reference planner that reads simulator state succeeds on the same states (.894 on the held-out pairings of scene 1), which shows that the input holds the information the task needs. Third, a causal cue test names the routing cue: we edit or move the candidate cue and the outcome follows the edit. A failure that misses the second condition belongs to the task, and we report it as a limit.

\textbf{Learned where-share.} For the repair columns we measure how much a network's behaviour depends on a given input. We replay recorded model inputs open loop and patch the where-input (the state token, or token plus crop) from a counterfactual run, in which the target is shifted, into the original run. The where-share is the fraction of the counterfactual effect on the output chunk that this patch recovers. A route passes when the lower bound of the pooled share is at least .70 and fails when the upper bound is under .30.

\textbf{Statistics.} Each confirmatory test has a pre-registered ledger row that fixes arms, states, predictions and decision rules before any main rollout. We compute every contrast by a paired bootstrap that resamples tasks and states jointly, with 2,000 draws, and report 95\% intervals. Held-out state sets are start states that no earlier experiment used; reads on other states are development reads, marked as such.

\section{The atlas: failures and their cues}
\label{sec:atlas}

This section lists what fails and which cue routes each failure, with each entry kept to its cue, its causal test and its effect (Appendix~\ref{app:L3} holds the full detail; Figure~\ref{fig:atlas} and Table~\ref{tab:B1} give the measured break of each failure on all five models).

\textbf{F1, the place follows a cue.} After a correct grasp, the carry and release follow a cue and ignore the instruction's target role. The excess failure on held-out states of scene 1 is +.450 [.28, .61] for \pifive, +.225 [.05, .43] for \pizero, +.500 [.26, .74] for GR00T and +.379 [.04, .73] for X-VLA. The cue test removes the cue by input edit: role views plus role phrases lift \pifive{} from .450 to .817 (Appendix~\ref{app:B}).

\textbf{F1m, the held outline starts the carry.} On \pifive, the high carry follows the outline of the held object. Painting the held bottle as its box raises N4+N6 from .625 to .812 (+.188 [+.075, +.325]) with no cost on trained tasks.

\textbf{F2, the habitual object is grasped.} The grasp excess is +.650 [.39, .88] on \pizero{} and +.833 [.67, .96] on SmolVLA. Role views alone lift \pizero{} by +.183 [+.021, +.412].

\textbf{F7, relational language is ignored.} On \pifive{} and GR00T the relational choice equals the ambiguous choice on 92.5 to 100\% of states. A resolver that turns the relation into one entity lifts \pifive{} from .40 to 1.00 on one family and from .20 to .775 on the negated one.

\textbf{F6, a covered target is lost.} A cylinder covers a displaced plate. The cover costs \pifive{} placement precision (cost .38 [.28, .48]) and GR00T the target's location (cost .60 [.51, .68]), and a control with the cylinder beside the plate keeps \pifive{} at .967.

\textbf{Scene 2 and a pre-registered cue law.} In scene 2, every model scores 0 of 720 episodes on the two held-out pairings. A pre-registered test on new layouts confirms a majority rule on \pifive: the place goes to the plate backed by two of three cues (nearer, more salient, trained), on .912 [.883, .942] of 445 placements in which the cues disagree. The views that repair scene 1 give .000 here, so an input edit removes only cues that the task does not need.

\textbf{A moved plate and a new layout.} Moving the plate in K1 .141 to .296 m away, frozen \pifive{} succeeds on .975 [.925, 1.000] of states while the narrow retrain R2 releases at the trained position on 31 of 40 and succeeds on 0 of 40, so narrow fine-tuning induces a position bias; RAA reaches the moved plate on .100, so a repair of one failure can cost robustness elsewhere (Appendices~\ref{app:F} and~\ref{app:L3}). A new scene-1 layout fails the scripted planners too, so we carry it as a limit (Appendix~\ref{app:B}).

\section{Where each failure sits: functions and a separable decomposition}
\label{sec:functions}

If a failure is a missing function, a module that owns the function should repair the failure and its removal should return it.

\textbf{Four functions.} F1 and F1m lack an owner of where the object goes and when it is let go. F2 lacks a binding from the named object role to the object that the gripper approaches. F7 lacks a resolution of a relation to one entity. F6 lacks a memory of a target across queries. In a standard VLA these functions share one path, from the VLM prefix to the action expert.

\textbf{The functions are spread through that path.} Activation patching on frozen \pifive{} writes one component at a time from a counterfactual run into the clean run (Appendix~\ref{app:D}). The effect spreads across many layers (largest single write .189 [.167, .209], participation ratio .155 [.133, .185] against a .2 criterion), no band of layers is selective, and 4 of 10 band-by-set cells are inverse. We report this as a negative result: the effect does not localise.

\textbf{A separable decomposition.} Three explicit modules own the functions. The resolver R turns a relational instruction such as ``the bowl on the left'' into one entity and serves F7. The pose memory M keeps the last estimate of an entity that a cover hides or a view loses and serves F6. The where-module serves F1 and F1m by aiming the arm at the release point of the target; Section~\ref{sec:modules} builds it. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the decomposition on the same frozen \pifive.

\section{Repairing inside the network}
\label{sec:inside}

If the missing function could be learned inside the network, the repair would be a training recipe. This section tests three recipes: retraining at matched data with a role channel, giving the network the exact target as an input at several data doses, and the same on \pizero, whose state pathway is trained.

\subsection{The matched-data ladder}
\label{sec:ladder}

We first train the binding into a policy on the same demonstrations as a plain retrain and test whether it closes the place gap. A ladder of retrains on LIBERO-Goal's eight known tasks uses the same 52,181 frames, the frozen VLM, the same action expert, 6,001 steps and one seed. R0 retrains the action expert on whole instructions and frames. R2 restricts the input to the active role's entity, with role phrases and painted views. Rrel is R0 plus the role channel, and RAA is R2 plus it. The channel is the position of the active role entity relative to the gripper, in whole centimetres, entered as a short text suffix on the prompt because the served \pifive{} reads no state input.

\textbf{Input purity repairs the grasp and the channel repairs part of the place.} R2 fixes the grasp (right-grasp rate .972) and places only .400 of those right grasps. RAA beats R2 on held-out pairings by +.267 [+.092, +.467], and its right-grasp rate rises by only .028, so the place step supplies almost all of the gain. The trained tasks stay level (+.086 [+.011, +.172]). The channel needs pure input: Rrel gains +.236 [\mi.006, +.553] over R0, and RAA beats Rrel by +.378 [+.133, +.658].

\textbf{The remaining gap sits at the release.} In 47 of 51 failures, RAA carries the right object to the right target and lets go outside LIBERO's placement tolerance, with misses of 3.7 to 7.9 cm against ReGuide's median of 0.9 to 1.7 cm. Every retrain that supplies the exact offset as numbers or text leaves a gap of centimetres, and a fixed servo term lifts RAA to .717 (RAA-s) and .878 (RAA-sL, two seeds: .878, .822), which raises the question of whether the network reads the number at all (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}, Appendices~\ref{app:H} and~\ref{app:L1}).

\subsection{The dose-by-servo grid on \pifive}
\label{sec:grid}

In RAA-sL, patching the servo input and the state token together accounts for .871 [.834, .914] of the total effect, and the servo alone carries .878. The state token carries \mi.007, so the trained \pifive{} expert does not read the value of the newly added token, and a second training seed reproduces this (\mi.002 [\mi.0024, \mi.0017]). Our shortcut hypothesis (Section~\ref{sec:intro}) predicts that a network uses a where-signal only as far as the data force it to and only if no other path supplies the correction. Four data routes test it, with one selection rule fixed before any run (Section~\ref{sec:setting}).

\textbf{Routes.} Route A relabels held-phase demonstration frames with a counterfactual goal offset of 1 to 9 cm on a servo-free model, so only the token can supply the shift. Route A0 is the same servo-free model with no relabel, the matched comparator. Route B adds a crop of the target as an extra image input and has no data dose. Route A+ adds planner demonstrations with shifts of 19 to 38 cm, which the prompt and the views do not determine, and keeps the servo. Route A+0 is A+ with the servo removed, added post hoc after A+'s probe (Table~\ref{tab:share}, Appendix~\ref{app:E}). A control with the same planner data, no token and no servo falls below RAA-sL by \mi.247 [\mi.483, \mi.030], so the planner data alone do not supply the where-skill.

\textbf{A newly added pathway stays unread without a dose, and the servo shuts it out.} With no data dose the share is zero (A0 and B). The relabel raises it by +.272 [+.238, +.312] over A0, and the crop adds nothing detectable (+.030 [\mi.034, +.089]). With the servo kept, even planner shifts of 19 to 38 cm leave the share at the reference value (A+ \mi.007). The servo accounts for that zero: A+0 reads .124, which is +.133 [+.096, +.171] above A+, and the large dose without the servo does not do better than the small relabel (A+0 minus A is \mi.150 [\mi.196, \mi.104]). No route reaches the .70 bar, and the fifth held-out state set stays sealed. Behaviour agrees: no servo-free route recovers RAA-sL's release aim on held-out pairings (A \mi.100 [\mi.261, +.050], B \mi.233 [\mi.447, \mi.019], A0 \mi.192 [\mi.406, +.019]), and A+0 reaches .603 against .878 for RAA-sL (Appendix~\ref{app:E}).

\textbf{An oracle: can the layer be learned when only the token decides?} The grid leaves open whether the new layer cannot be learned or the data never demand it. On 131 observations on 23 layouts that differ in the target's table spot by at least 15 cm, so that only the token names the spot, A+0 and A follow the token (follow share $\varphi$ .771 [.605, .887] and .662 [.542, .783], against \mi.001 for A0; neither lower bound reaches .70), while with the servo kept the network follows it on no frame (A+ \mi.068, RAA-sL \mi.006). The layer can be learned when the data force it, and the servo carries the where. An arm with 39 more paired-spot demonstrations reaches .777 [.628, .873], level with A+0 (Table~\ref{tab:L1}, Appendices~\ref{app:L4} and~\ref{app:K}).

\subsection{\pizero: the network reads the target and still fails more pairings}
\label{sec:pi0}

The \pifive{} null concerns a pathway that we add, so we repeat the test on \pizero, which reads state natively. We put the same token into its trained state dimensions 0 to 3 and train two arms: servo kept, and servo off with no added data (Table~\ref{tab:L2}, Appendix~\ref{app:L4}; Section~\ref{sec:limits} lists the deviations from \pizero{} as shipped). Both arms pass the fit gate on the trained tasks.

\textbf{The \pifive{} null is specific to a newly added pathway.} With the servo off, \pizero{} reads the token at .542 [.478, .609], which is +.531 [+.463, +.607] above \pifive's A0. With the servo kept, the share falls to \mi.150, so the servo suppresses the token here too.

\textbf{Reading the token does not give control.} The servo-off arm scores .506 on held-out pairings against .761 with the servo kept, a difference of \mi.256 [\mi.386, \mi.128], with the trained tasks intact (+.094 [+.003, +.208], non-inferior at the margin of \mi.05). A rule we fixed before the rollouts holds. We call this pattern read but not in control. It extends the finding of \citet{jiang2610} from internal representations to an explicit input: the network reads the exact target and still places worse than a network with an explicit owner of the where.

\section{Repairing with explicit modules}
\label{sec:modules}

If the network does not own the where, a module must. We give a module sole ownership of the target-directed motion of the same RAA-sL checkpoint at serving time and train nothing. The module owns where the object goes and when it is let go, and the expert keeps how to move: the lift, the approach, the final alignment and the gripper. The final design, W5, aims at the release point that the demonstrations show for the target's class and hands the last 3 cm back to the expert; it took six variants, W1 to W6 (Appendix~\ref{app:L5}).

\input{parts/matrix}

\textbf{The servo takes over the function.} Keeping the servo drives the token's share to zero on \pifive{} (A+ \mi.007 against A+0 .124; oracle frames \mi.068 against .771) and cuts it on \pizero{} from .542 to \mi.150. Both backbones, and both seeds of the main retrain (\mi.007 and \mi.002), show the same takeover: once an explicit owner supplies the correction, the network stops using the token.

\textbf{The servo alone loses the covered target, and the pose memory supplies it.} A servo aims at the entity's last estimate and cannot follow a plate that a cover hides. The covered-plate cell F6\_b scores .000 for \pizero{} with the servo against .200 with it off, and .017 for RAA-sL against .383 for A0 on \pifive. W5 with the pose memory scores .817 on the covered plate against .050 for RAA-sL on the third held-out set, and removing the memory costs .217, .400 and .367 on the three sets (Table~\ref{tab:removal}).

W5's weakest cell, N4, sits below ReGuide on two of three sets. The servo is hand-set, and a target class absent from the demonstrations cannot be aimed (Appendix~\ref{app:L5}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Privileged inputs.} The fixes need depth and calibration: the estimator that locates entities uses rendered depth and calibration, and ReGuide's perceived-pose runs use the same perception. Scene-2 role views use simulator masks, and the scene-2 where channel is privileged, with a channel error of 0 by construction. The F7 resolver is a fixed-template rule on a parsed scene, with no language-understanding claim, and the reference planners use simulator state or privileged depth. A calibrated RGB-D sensor would supply these inputs, and an RGB-only policy would not.

\textbf{The \pizero{} arms.} Our \pizero{} arms deviate from \pizero{} as shipped: the token enters the trained state dimensions 0 to 3, whose live columns were trained on end-effector position and one rotation component, and the arms use quantile normalisation and no delta actions. The negative share of the servo-kept arm (\mi.150) is leftover dependence on those columns. The oracle test set and ORC are smaller than planned (Appendix~\ref{app:K6}), and the \pizero{} results rest on single seeds on development states, so the .256 gap is a single sample.

\textbf{Baselines, statistics and scale.} Our ReGuide is a reimplementation with our segmenter, and we ran no arms on the CRAFT, ACT-VLA or AC-VLA splits, so our numbers are not directly comparable with theirs (Appendix~\ref{app:L0}). Every trained policy has one seed, except RAA-sL, which has two (Appendix~\ref{app:J}). The seed gaps on held-out pairings (.056) and on trained tasks (.081) are single samples of the seed spread, so success differences of that size between single-seed policies are within seed noise. The designs W1 to W5 came from diagnoses on development states, and the held-out sets are free of that selection. The registered tests apply no multiplicity correction. The atlas runs mostly on development states, and only F1 and F2 replicate on held-out states.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

VLAs fail new pairings of known parts in a small number of identifiable ways. A cue routes each failure, and each sits in a function that a standard VLA spreads through one learned network. Repairs inside the network fall short: more data and the target as an input leave a gap even when the network reads the target (\pizero{} reads it at .542 and trails its servo-kept twin by .256), and on \pifive{} a new input pathway stays unread unless the data force it. Explicit modules that own the function repair the failure and take it over: a where-module inside a retrained policy and ReGuide outside a frozen one converge at .89 to .92, and removing the resolver or the memory returns exactly its failure. The necessity of an explicit owner is an empirical claim over our tests, in one simulator and with single seeds.

\section*{Impact Statement}

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine Learning. Robot policies that fail on new combinations of instructions, objects and targets can act in ways that a user did not intend, such as carrying an object to the wrong place, and such failures matter for the safety of robots that work near people. Our atlas helps practitioners locate and test for these failures before deployment, and our repairs depend on privileged perception that a deployed system may lack. A policy that passes our tests in simulation is not certified safe on a real robot. We see no further societal consequences that we must highlight here.
